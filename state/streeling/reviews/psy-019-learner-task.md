# PSY-019 learner task — no answer key

You have read **Combining Forecasters Who Share a Source**. Complete this transfer task without opening the course answer blocks. The team below is hypothetical: it is not Streeling data and not a forecast.

Eight engineers each estimate, in weeks, how long a project will take; the project is then done and its real duration is recorded. An error is the estimate minus the real duration. Over many past projects, each engineer's errors have a mean of −3 weeks (all eight are optimistic by the same amount) and a standard deviation of 6 weeks around that mean. The errors of any two engineers have a correlation of 0.25, partly because all eight read the same specification. The team's estimate is the plain average of the eight.

1. Compute the mean squared error of one engineer's estimate and of the team's estimate, and the square root of each, in weeks. Keep the bias and the variance apart in your working.
2. Leave the bias aside. What is the lowest variance the team's estimate can reach, however many engineers of this kind join? How many independent engineers is the team of eight worth? What is the smallest team of this kind that is worth three independent engineers, and can a team of this kind be worth four?
3. Still leaving the bias aside, a ninth estimate is added. Compute the variance of the new average, and the percentage by which the variance falls, when the ninth is (a) one more engineer of the same kind; (b) an outside estimator with the same standard deviation and errors uncorrelated with the team's (assume one exists), given one vote in nine; (c) the same outsider, given the weight that minimises the variance. Give that weight.
4. Bring the bias back. What share of the team's mean squared error does it account for? Compare two actions: growing the team from 8 to 80 engineers of the same kind, and subtracting the bias (take it as known exactly). Give the mean squared error and the percentage drop for each.
5. The team lead says: "On the projects where our eight estimates are close together, the average can be trusted." (a) Compute the expected spread of the eight estimates around their average, and its square root in weeks. Which parts of an error does the spread contain: the bias, the common part, the individual parts? (b) Write a plan, fixed before any new data arrive, to estimate the correlation between the engineers' errors from finished projects: what to record, the estimate to compute, what to do about the bias, and one result that would falsify the claim "the eight are worth about three independent engineers". Say what your plan cannot establish.

Return your calculations, your plan and your uncertainty. Write "not assessed" where evidence is absent. The reviewer scores the unedited response separately; this task contains no answers.
