# Feedback Delay: Correct the State You Actually Observed

> **Department of Cybernetics** | Intermediate | 40 minutes

## Objectives

- Separate action-to-observation delay, transport delay, and sample age.
- Reconstruct a delayed discrete response from a specified history.
- Demonstrate a gain that succeeds without delay but fails with delay.
- Prevent an old or duplicate observation from justifying another correction.

## 1. Three timestamps answer three different questions

Suppose a synthetic command is issued at monotonic time 0 ms, its effect is measured at 100 ms, and the measurement reaches the controller at 400 ms. Action-to-observation delay is 100 ms. Observation-to-receipt transport delay is 300 ms. At receipt, the sample is already 300 ms old, and the full command-to-feedback delay is 400 ms. These are elapsed times from one clock origin, not unrelated wall-clock timestamps.

NI's controller documentation identifies delayed observation or actuator response as sources of deadtime. [Primary reference: NI](https://www.ni.com/en/shop/labview/pid-theory-explained.html). In software, a queued job, delayed status hook, or stale read can create analogous timing problems. The analogy does not assign a numerical plant model to an agent.

An acceptance receipt may arrive quickly while the requested outcome takes longer. The verified continuation incident had distinct acceptance and durable completion evidence. Treating acceptance latency as completion latency would substitute a different event into the timing calculation. Missing completion evidence means that completion delay is not yet measured.

### Practice exercise and worked correction

The controller makes its decision at 450 ms using the same observation. Calculate sample age and command-to-decision time. Does rereading the stored message at 450 reset its age?

**Correction:** Age is `450-100 = 350 ms`; command-to-decision time is 450 ms. Rereading does not create a new measurement. The receipt or download timestamp must not overwrite the observation timestamp. A correct answer names the event each interval spans.

## 2. Delay changes the recurrence

Use a synthetic plant `x[k+1] = x[k] + K*(1-y[k])`, starting at zero. With no delay, `y[k]=x[k]`. With two ticks of measurement delay, `y[k]=x[k-2]`. Define pre-start history as zero. There is no saturation, random noise, external disturbance, or changing gain. One tick is an arbitrary fixed simulation interval.

At gain 0.8 without delay, states are 0, 0.8, 0.96, 0.992, and 0.9984. With two delayed ticks, the first three controls each see zero, so states become 0, 0.8, 1.6, and 2.4. At tick three the controller finally sees `x[1]=0.8`, adds 0.16, and reaches 2.56. It subsequently reacts to earlier high values after the state has already moved again.

The delayed error obeys `e[k+1] = e[k] - K*e[k-2]`. CYB-004's simpler relation `e[k+1]=(1-K)e[k]` no longer applies. Reusing its stability interval without checking the model would erase the variable that this lesson is investigating.

### Practice exercise and worked correction

Calculate delayed states at ticks five and six from the trace above. Explain why the controller is still moving after the target has been crossed.

**Correction:** At tick four it sees `x[2]=1.6`, adds -0.48, and reaches `x[5]=2.08`. At tick five it sees `x[3]=2.4`, adds -1.12, reaching `x[6]=0.96`. The action depends on an older state. Overshoot is not evidence of a new disturbance here; it is generated by the specified delay and gain.

## 3. A counterexample is narrower than a tuning rule

The fixture simulates 60 ticks. Gain 0.8 with immediate feedback finishes with floating-point error reported as zero. The same gain with two delayed ticks finishes with absolute error about 108.4166. Reducing gain to 0.2 with the same delay yields final error about `3.38e-9`. Its first states are 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.76, 0.88, and 0.96, followed by a small overshoot.

This is a concrete counterexample to “a gain that works without delay will work with delay.” It is not a proof that 0.2 stabilizes every process or that lower gain always solves delay. Delayed discrete models can be analyzed through their characteristic roots, but this lesson establishes a finite-horizon result rather than a general theorem.

Do not repair a slow operational loop by repeatedly submitting new work before existing work can change the measured outcome. Waiting alone is also insufficient: a blocked worker may never improve. Use a bounded observation schedule, inspect genuine progress or an explicit blocker, and keep a stop condition. Rate limiting and backoff are developed separately in CYB-009.

### Practice exercise and worked correction

A reviewer says “the experiment proves low-gain coordination is stable.” Rewrite that sentence with the exact model, horizon, and evidence boundary.

**Correction:** “For the zero-history scalar integrator with two-tick measurement delay and no saturation or disturbance, gain 0.2 produced absolute target error below 0.001 after 60 ticks.” Add that no live coordinator was tested. This wording preserves a useful result without upgrading it to fleet behavior.

### Practice exercise and worked correction

Last accepted sample is sequence 17 observed at 100. At time 401, sequence 17 is delivered again. At 410, sequence 18 arrives but was observed at 95. What should the controller do?

**Correction:** Reject the first as a duplicate. Investigate or reject the second under a monotonic-observation contract: its higher sequence does not explain its older observation time. Keep reasons and original timestamps. A new envelope cannot justify a fresh correction when the underlying measurement is invalid.
